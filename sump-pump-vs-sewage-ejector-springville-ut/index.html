Sump Pump vs Sewage Ejector Pump in Springville, UT

A sump pump removes groundwater and rainwater from a pit, while a sewage ejector pump lifts wastewater, including solids, from basement toilets, showers and sinks up to the main sewer line. They look alike but do different jobs and must not be swapped.

This guide explains what each does, how the pits differ and where Springville code comes in.

Sump pump and ejector pump compared

The differences matter for safety and code.

Sump and ejector pumps
FeatureSump pumpSewage ejector pump
HandlesGroundwater and rain, mostly clear waterToilet, shower and sink waste with solids
BasinOpen or lidded pitSealed basin with a vent

SolidsSmall grit onlyDesigned to pass solids

Why basements need an ejector

A toilet in a basement drains by gravity only if the sewer main sits below the floor. In many houses the main leaves the house higher than the basement floor. A sewage ejector basin catches the waste and a pump lifts it to the main when the basin fills.

The basin is sealed and vented so gas does not enter the room. A check valve keeps waste from flowing back. If the pump fails, waste can back up into the basement, so the system deserves regular testing and a high water alarm.

What Springville code says about sumps

Springville's municipal code, section 4-13-328, says a sump must be city approved. Section 4-13-202(2)(h) says foundation or footing drains, and crawl space or basement pump discharges, are allowed only with written city approval. These rules apply to groundwater pumps, not to sewage ejectors, which connect to the sanitary sewer through plumbing that the permit and inspection process covers.

The city's basement guide says adding plumbing to a basement requires a permit. Call the Building Division at 801-489-2704 to confirm what applies to your project before you buy equipment.

Deciding which one you need

Start with what drains into the pit.

Clear groundwater or rain

You need a sump pump with an approved discharge line.

Waste from fixtures

You need a sewage ejector in a sealed vented basin.

Both problems

You need two separate systems. Never combine them in one pit.

Unsure

Have a plumber trace what feeds the pit before you buy anything.

Maintenance for each

Test a sump pump before spring snowmelt and again in fall by pouring water into the pit. Check the float, the check valve and the discharge outlet. Clean debris from the pit. Replace a backup battery on the maker schedule.

For an ejector, listen for the pump cycling normally and watch for odors. A gurgling sound after a flush can point to a failing check valve or a vent problem. Because waste is involved, call a plumber for service rather than opening the basin yourself.

Signs something is wrong

A sump that runs constantly may have a stuck float or a short discharge line. A pit that overflows during storms may need a larger pump or a backup. A sewage smell in the basement points to an unsealed basin lid or a failed vent. Water coming up a basement floor drain is serious.

Our sump pump page covers service on pit systems, and sewer line repair covers problems with the main line itself. For routing a sump discharge, call the city first.

Spring snowmelt and Hobble Creek

Springville noted high spring flows in Hobble Creek in 2023 from heavy snowmelt, and groundwater can rise with it. A basement that stays dry through a dry fall may take water in April. A well sized sump with a good backup is cheap protection compared with flood damage.

Homes in Mapleton and Provo face different soils and water tables, so ask a local plumber about your lot.

Installation basics for each system

A sump installation starts with a pit cut into the slab, set with gravel at the base and lined with a basin. The pump sits on a flat support, the discharge pipe rises with a check valve and the outlet runs outdoors on an approved route. A lid keeps debris out of the room.

An ejector installation starts with a sealed basin that receives the drain lines from basement fixtures. A vent line leaves the basin and ties into the home venting system. The discharge line carries waste up to the main with a check valve and a union to make service easier. Both jobs involve concrete work, so plan for dust and a few days of disruption.

Questions to ask before you buy

Ask the installer how deep the basin will be, where the discharge will go and whether the city has approved it. Ask what happens during a power outage. Ask for a written scope that lists each part. Clear answers show the job has been thought through.

Sealed lids and odors

Sealed lids and vents also keep odors out of the house. If you notice a sewage smell in a finished basement, check the lid seal and the vent first. Do not assume the smell is harmless, since sewer gas should not be inside a living space.

Backup power and alarms

A pump that cannot run during a power outage cannot protect the basement. A battery backup or a water powered backup keeps a sump working. For an ejector, a high water alarm tells you when the basin is filling faster than the pump empties it. In both cases, test the system on a schedule, since a backup that has never been tested is only a hope.

Cost factors, not prices

Pump type, basin depth, slab cutting, discharge routing and electrical work all affect the price. An ejector installation costs more because of the sealed basin, vent and check valves. We explain the price before work starts, based on what we see in your basement.

Code and permit note

The International Plumbing Code (IPC) calls for sealed and vented basins for sewage ejectors, which is a key difference from a sump pit.
