Tank or Tankless Water Heater for a Springville, UT Home

Choose a tank if you want a simple like for like replacement and a lower upfront cost, and choose tankless if you want hot water on demand and have enough gas or electric supply. For most Springville households the deciding factors are how many fixtures run at once and what your gas line can carry.

Below is how to compare them honestly, with the maintenance each one needs.

Side by side

Both work well in the right house.

Tankless suits

  • Households that want hot water without waiting for a tank to refill
  • Wall mounting to save floor space
  • Homes with adequate gas or electric service
  • Owners who will flush yearly

A tank suits

  • Simple replacement in the same spot
  • Lower upfront cost
  • Limited gas line or panel capacity
  • Households that run many fixtures at the same time

The question that decides it: flow

A tankless unit is sized by how much water it can heat per minute and by how much it raises the temperature. In cold months, incoming water is colder, so the same unit delivers less hot water. Springville's January mean low of 22 degrees Fahrenheit means cold ground and cold incoming water, so size for winter, not summer.

Count the fixtures that can run together. Two showers and a dishwasher need more than a single shower. Your installer should ask this before quoting a model.

Water quality in Springville

The city draws from four springs and seven wells, and the 2024 report lists total dissolved solids from 240 to 476 ppm. It does not report hardness. Scale is the main enemy of both tank and tankless heaters, so test your tap before you choose, and ask the Water Division at 801-489-2740 if you want guidance.

Gas supply and venting

Gas tankless units often need a larger gas line than a tank, plus a vent made for their exhaust. If your meter or line is undersized, upgrading adds cost. Electric tankless units need heavy circuits that many panels cannot spare. We check both before recommending anything, and our tankless service page and installation page explain what to expect.

Space, noise and location

Tankless units hang on a wall and need clearance for the vent, the service valves and the front panel. Gas models need a vent path to the outside, so the location often moves closer to an exterior wall. Electric models can sit nearly anywhere but need a heavy circuit.

Units make some noise while heating, mostly fan and burner sound. Avoid a wall shared with a bedroom. In a cold garage, check that the unit and its drain are rated for freezing weather, and that condensate from high efficiency models cannot freeze in its line.

What the city says about permits

Springville's published permit information does not say whether a water heater swap needs a permit, but it does list required inspections for gas lines. Call the Building Division at 801-489-2704 to confirm what applies to your project.

Sizing for a Springville winter

Incoming water temperature changes with the seasons. In winter, the water in the ground is colder, so a tankless unit has more temperature to add and delivers less flow at the same setting. A unit that feels generous in August may struggle in January.

Ask the installer to size the unit using winter incoming temperature and the number of fixtures you expect to run together. If you have a large soaking tub, a recirculating loop or a body spray shower, mention it, since these draw a lot of water at once.

What drives running cost

Running cost depends on the fuel, the unit efficiency and how you use hot water. Tankless units avoid standby loss, the heat a tank loses while it sits. Households that use little hot water save more from that. Households with constant use see less difference.

Because gas and electric rates change, we do not quote savings numbers here. Ask the installer for the unit's efficiency rating and use your own utility rates to compare. Also count maintenance, since a yearly flush is part of owning a tankless heater.

A short decision checklist

Work through these in order.

Count your fixtures

List the bathrooms, the kitchen and the laundry, and note which can run together.

Check your supply

Look at your gas meter and line size, or your electrical panel space.

Test your water

Use a kit or ask the Water Division at 801-489-2740, since hardness decides maintenance.

Decide on tradeoffs

Weigh upfront cost, floor space and yearly maintenance.

Problems owners report after switching

Cold water sandwiches, error codes and rumbling noises top the list. A rumble during heating often means scale on the heat exchanger, which a flush clears. An error code usually names a sensor, ignition or venting fault, and the manual lists it. Write the code down before calling.

Lower than expected flow can mean clogged inlet filters or low gas pressure. Check the filters first, since they are easy to clean. If a unit shuts down with a code for blocked venting, stop using it and call, because exhaust gases must have a clear path outside.

If your tank has already failed

A leaking tank cannot be repaired. See water heater replacement for emergency swaps. Planned changes to tankless are better made before a failure, when you have time to upgrade gas lines and venting.
