When a clogged drain is an emergency

Call right away if a toilet, tub or floor drain is backing up, if more than one fixture is slow at once, or if water is rising toward carpet or drywall. One slow bathroom sink can usually wait for a same-day visit when the schedule allows, but a full main line cannot.

Stop adding water

Pause the dishwasher and washer, skip showers and hold off on flushing. Every gallon you add has nowhere to go if the main line is blocked.

Check the lowest drain

A basement floor drain or tub that gurgles when you flush upstairs points to the main line. Tell the dispatcher what you see, because it decides which machine goes on the truck.

Skip the chemical opener

Caustic openers sit in a standing clog, soften older pipe joints and can splash on whoever opens the trap next. Plain hot tap water is as far as a homeowner should go.

Protect the room

Move rugs and stored boxes off the floor near the drain and keep anything plugged in away from wet areas. Do not wade through standing water near outlets.

What your symptoms say about the clog

The pattern of slow fixtures tells a plumber where to start before any tool touches the pipe.

One sink or tub is slow

The clog is almost always in that fixture's own trap or branch line. Hair, soap scum and food waste build up within a few feet of the drain, so a cable or a trap cleaning usually clears it.

Several fixtures gurgle

A shared branch or the main line is restricted. Air pushed ahead of the water makes toilets burp and tub drains gurgle when the washer or a sink drains upstairs.

Water backs up at the lowest drain

A basement floor drain, a laundry standpipe or a ground floor tub filling with gray water means the main line is blocked downstream. Stop all water use and call.

A sewer smell that comes and goes

Smell with no backup can mean a dry trap, but a smell that returns along with slow drains points to buildup holding waste in the pipe. A camera shows which.

How we clear a drain

Every visit follows the same order, so the work stays proportionate to the problem.

Locate the stoppage

We run water, test fixtures one at a time and check the cleanouts to decide whether the clog sits in a fixture, a branch or the main line.

Cut or flush it clear

A drum machine or hand auger with the right cutting head opens hair, paper and light roots. Grease and heavy scale call for a jetter, covered on our hydro jetting page.

Look inside the pipe

When a clog returns or the pipe is old, a camera shows whether the cause is a sag, a broken joint or roots. Our sewer camera inspection page explains what the picture shows.

Test and explain

We run water at every affected fixture, confirm the flow and tell you what caused the blockage so it does not come back.

Cable, jetter or camera: which method fits

No single tool is right for every clog. Matching the method to the blockage keeps the pipe intact and the bill honest.

Why Springville drains clog

Most Springville clogs come from what goes down the drain, not from the city system. Cooking grease cools and coats kitchen lines, wipes sold as flushable do not break up the way paper does, and hair builds in showers on every street from Main Street to the Hobble Creek side of town.

Finished basements add a local wrinkle. Springville's own basement permit guide lists keeping 18 inches of clearance at plumbing cleanouts after drywall goes up. A cleanout boxed in behind a wall turns a quick cable job into an access problem, so check where yours is before you finish a basement and ask the Building Division at 801-489-2704 what applies to your project.

Code and permit note

Code note: drain lines are laid out under the International Plumbing Code (IPC) rules for traps, slope and venting, which is why a vent or slope fault can mimic a clog.
