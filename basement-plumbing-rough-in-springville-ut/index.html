Basement Plumbing Rough In and Permits in Springville, UT

Before you add a bathroom, wet bar or laundry to a Springville basement, you need a permit. The city says finishing a basement or adding plumbing requires an application, plan review that takes about three weeks, and inspections before walls are closed.

This guide lays out the order of work, the details inspectors look for and the plumbing choices that matter most below grade.

What Springville requires

According to the city's published basement guide, finishing an unfinished basement or adding plumbing needs a permit before work starts. Plan review takes roughly three weeks. The permit checklist lists inspections for underground plumbing, the sewer lateral, a four way inspection that includes rough plumbing and a gas line inspection, scheduled through the City Inspect portal with 24 hours of notice.

The guide also lists items that often fail: nail plates on pipes within 1 1/4 inches of the stud face and 18 inches of clearance at cleanouts after drywall. The Building Division, 801-489-2704, is open Monday to Friday, 8 to 1.

The order of work

Skipping a step costs time and money.

Plan and apply

Draw the layout, locate the main drain and file for the permit. Plan review takes about three weeks.

Underground plumbing

If the slab is cut for new drains, the underground work is inspected before concrete goes back.

Rough in

Supply, drain and vent pipes go in, then the rough plumbing is inspected before walls close.

Finish

After framing and drywall, fixtures are set and the final inspection is scheduled.

Drains below the sewer: gravity or pump

A basement bathroom needs a way to drain. If the main sewer line sits below the basement floor, a toilet can drain by gravity after cutting the slab. If the sewer sits higher than the new fixtures, you need a sewage ejector, which pumps waste up to the main.

That decision changes cost and layout. Ask the plumber to find the depth of your main line first. Our sump pump page explains the difference between sump and ejector systems.

Cleanouts and access

A cleanout is a capped pipe that gives access to the drain for clearing a blockage. The city guide calls for 18 inches of clearance at cleanouts after drywall, so plan drywall and trim around them. If a cleanout sits behind a closed wall, a simple clog turns into a demolition job.

Place access panels for shutoffs and any mixing valves too. A finished basement should still let a plumber reach the main water shutoff and the cleanouts without tools.

Nail plates and protection

Pipes close to the face of a stud can be hit by drywall screws or nails. The city guide calls for nail plates when a pipe sits within 1 1/4 inches of the stud face. A cheap steel plate protects against a punctured line that may not show up until the wall is finished.

Venting is the part people forget

Every fixture drain needs a vent so wastewater can flow without gurgling or siphoning the trap dry. In a basement, vent pipes must travel up through the walls and out the roof or tie into an existing vent at the right height. Running them is easiest before walls are closed, which is one reason the rough in comes first.

If a vent is missing or undersized, symptoms include slow drains, bubbling toilets and sewer smell from traps. Those problems are costly to fix once drywall is up. Inspectors check vents at the rough in stage, so ask your plumber to walk you through where each one runs.

Realistic scheduling

Add up the pieces: about three weeks of plan review, the time to cut and pour a slab patch, the days waiting on inspections and then finish work. A basement bathroom can easily stretch across a couple of months from application to final inspection. Starting the permit early is the best single time saver.

Schedule inspections as soon as the work is ready, since the city asks for 24 hours of notice. Keep the work visible and accessible until the inspector signs off, and keep the approved plans on site so questions are answered quickly.

Choosing materials that last below grade

Basement pipes face moisture, cold slab and sometimes chemical exposure from concrete cure. PVC and ABS resist corrosion in drains. For supply lines, PEX tolerates cold and moves with temperature changes. Insulate supply lines along exterior walls so a cold snap does not freeze them behind new drywall.

Use shutoff valves at each fixture group so a repair does not require shutting the whole house off. Label the shutoffs on the wall before it closes. Sleeve pipes where they pass through a slab or foundation wall so concrete movement does not crimp them.

What to ask the plumber before signing

Ask who pulls the permit and who schedules the inspections. Ask whether the price includes cutting and patching the slab. Ask what happens if the inspector fails the rough in. A clear answer to each lowers the chance of surprises, and our plumbing installation page lists the kind of project plan we give you.

Water and moisture first

Before finishing a basement, deal with water entry. A new bathroom on a floor that takes water every spring is a bad plan. See basement waterproofing for sumps and drains, and ask a plumber about any dampness before you frame.

Homes in Spanish Fork and Mapleton follow their own city rules, so check with those offices for projects there.

Code and permit note

Rough in inspections check work against the International Plumbing Code (IPC) as Utah amends it, including venting, slope and supports.
